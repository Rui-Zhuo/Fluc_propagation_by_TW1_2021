ABSTRACT
Wave reflection is a fundamental process in turbulence theories of the solar wind but has lacked23
direct observational evidence in the solar wind acceleration region. Using coordinated multi-station24
radio sounding of the Tianwen-1 spacecraft, we identify inward-propagating compressive disturbances25
coexisting with outward-propagating fluctuations at 5.7 and 12.3R ⊙. The outward and inward26
components approximately satisfy the kinematic relation expected for wave reflection, while magneto-27
hydrodynamic simulations reproduce the observed propagation reversal and localized phase structures28
associated with wave compression and rarefaction through reflection by density inhomogeneities.29
Together, these independent signatures constitute the first remote-sensing evidence for compressive-30
wave reflection in the solar wind acceleration region, provide new constraints on wave–turbulence31
interactions relevant to solar wind acceleration.32
33
1.INTRODUCTION34
The solar wind acceleration region, extending from a few to several tens of solar radii, is the key interface where35
mass, momentum, and energy are transferred from the solar corona into the heliosphere (P. E.N. 1958; S. R. Cranmer36
& A. R. Winebarger 2019). Despite decades of theoretical and observational efforts, how the solar wind continues to37
gain energy and accelerate through the outer corona remains one of the central unresolved problems in heliophysics38
(S. R. Cranmer & A. R. Winebarger 2019; N. M. Viall & J. E. Borovsky 2020).39
40
Wave–turbulence driven models have emerged as one of the leading theoretical frameworks (S. R. Cranmer & A. R.41
Winebarger 2019; J. C. Perez et al. 2021). In this scenario, the gradients in density and Alfv´ en speed generate partial42

background magnetic field, respectively (M. Krogulec et al. 1994; A. Kumar et al. 2025; R. Meyrand et al. 2025). Their44
nonlinear interaction drives the turbulent cascade that transfers energy from large scales to smaller scales. Parametric45
decay instability (PDI) is another promising mechanism linking Alfv´ en-wave dynamics to the generation of compressive46

and compressive mode) (C. A. Gonz´ alez et al. 2023). The instability manifests as energy exchange between Alf´ een48
waves and magnetosonic waves, thereby amplifying turbulence cascades and facilitating effective energy transport.49
50
Direct measurements by Parker Solar Probe (PSP) have provided the first in situ characterization of the inward51
Els¨ asser component (z−) in the near-Sun solar wind (C. H. K. Chen et al. 2020; C. C. Chaston et al. 2020). Although52
the turbulence is strongly dominated by outward fluctuations (z+), a finitez − component is consistently observed. The53
Els¨ asser energies ofz− have a much shallower radial variation (|δz −|2 ∝r −0.51±0.11) than the outward-propagating54
ones (|δz +|2 ∝r −1.72±0.12) between 0.17 (35R ⊙)∼1 AU. The radial variation of the inward component is consistent55
with its generation by reflection from the gradient in Alfv´ en speed (C. H. K. Chen et al. 2020; B. D. G. Chandran &56
J. C. Perez 2019). While alternative mechanisms, including PDI and local nonlinear process, have also been proposed57
(C. H. K. Chen et al. 2020; L. Yang et al. 2023). Recent theoretical work suggests that part of the observedz − popula-58
tion may represent anomalous fluctuations that co-propagate with the dominantz + component rather than genuinely59
sunward-propagating Alfv´ en waves (L. Yang et al. 2023). Because PSP provides only single-point measurements,60
distinguishing propagation from temporal evolution remains challenging (W. H. Matthaeus et al. 2019), highlighting61
the need for spatially separated multi-point sampling to separate spatial from temporal variations.62
63
Previous observations have established MHD wave reflection throughout the low solar atmosphere, including fast-64
mode waves at coronal-hole boundaries observed in EUV(X. Zhou et al. 2022), slow magnetoacoustic waves in coronal65
loops (S. Mandal et al. 2016), and, more recently, Alfv´ en waves in the chromosphere (M. Murabito et al. 2024). How-66
ever, whether such reflection can be directly observed in the solar wind acceleration region remains largely unknown,67
because the tenuous plasma produces only weak emission, making reflected wave fronts difficult to image.68
69
Multi-station radio sounding provides a simultaneous sampling at multiple locations, substantially reducing the70
ambiguity between spatial structure and temporal evolution. Radio signals transmitted by deep-space spacecraft71
undergo phase and frequency fluctuations as they traverse electron-density irregularities (D. Wexler et al. 2020), which72
are generally interpreted as signatures of compressive plasma disturbances (S. Chiba et al. 2025). Based on Taylor’s73
frozen-in approximation (G. I. Taylor 1938), multi-station cross-correlation techniques have been developed to study74
the propagation speed of these density irregularities ( N.A.Armand et al. 2003; O. I. Yakovlev et al. 1989). More75
recently, geographically distributed multi VLBI stations have extended this approach to resolve the propagation of76
solar wind streams, streamer waves, and CMEs (M. Ma et al. 2022; Z. Wang et al. 2023).77
78
This paper report the wave reflection signatures during the solar wind acceleration region detected by the Multi-79
station radio sounding. Section 2 introduces the observation, compressive-fluctuation propagation measurements and80
MHD modeling of wave reflection. Section 3 identify the wave-reflection signatures from measured propagation, phase81
fluctuations, and theoretical modeling. Section 4 is the discusses and concludes.82

2.1.Multi-station Observing Campaign Design and Execution for Solar Conjunction85
Focusing on the 2021 September-October solar conjunction, we specifically designed, coordinated, and executed an86
intensive multi-station radio-sounding campaign, conducting 18 dedicated observations of the Tianwen-1 spacecraft.87
The campaign leveraged antennas from the European VLBI Network (W. Schl¨ uter & D. Behrend 2007), Russian88
VLBI Network (N. Shuygina et al. 2019), Chinese VLBI Network (Q. H. Liu et al. 2022), University of Tasmania89
(G. M. Calv´ es et al. 2021; J. Edwards et al. 2025), and Chinese Kashi and Jiamusi deep-space stations (R. Zhang90
et al. 2022)(Fig. 1a, Table 1 in Appendix A).91

For each observing station, the piercing-point (the closest approach point of the spacecraft radio ray to the Sun)97
(M. Xiong et al. 2023) was determined and transformed into the Carrington coordinate system (W. T. Barnes et al.98
2020). The projected baseline is defined as the line connecting piercing-points (Fig. 1b). Since the piercing points99
are located far from the Sun, their radial directions are approximately parallel (Fig. 1c). Based on the orientation of100
the projected baseline, the baselines are classified into three categories: quasi-radial, for which the angle ∆θbetween101
the baseline and the radial direction is less than 30 ◦; oblique, 30 ◦∼60◦; and transverse, 60 ◦∼90◦. In Fig. 1b, when102
the piercing-point located at 5.7R ⊙ off the center of sun on 4 October 2021, four radio telescopes took part in the103
observation. The Js-Bd and Yg-Hh pairs form two adjacent quasi-radial baselines with mean radial separations of104
∼853 km and∼4600 km, respectively. Their difference in transverse distance is around 4000 km. Js–Hh and Bd–Hh105
are oblique baselines (38.2 ◦,46.8 ◦ respectively ), while Bd–Yg is transverse baselines (∆θ∼81.7 ◦). These projected106
baselines, spanning different orientations and spatial separations on the order of thousands km, allow the spatial107
evolution and scale-dependent coherence of compressive fluctuations to be examined.108

2.2.Multi-point Characterization of Compressive Fluctuation Propagation110
Solar-wind density fluctuations exhibit structures over a broad range of spatial and temporal scales, reflecting the111
intrinsically multi-scale nature of solar-wind turbulence (D. Verscharen et al. 2019). The space–time correlation analy-112
sis used in multi-point measurement of the fluctuations, is able to study the bulk-flow speed, and the coupling between113
the spatial structure and temporal evolution (M. Xiong et al. 2023). The linear-wave propagation model including114
random sweeping effects is proposed to describe the space–time correlationR(r, τ) in compressible turbulence (D. Li115
et al. 2013).R(r, τ) can reflect not only convection by the mean flow but also intrinsic wave propagation, and random116
sweeping leads to the decorrelation of fluctuations.117
118
Here, we characterize the propagation of the observed frequency fluctuations using three complementary approaches:119
individual spike tracking (M. Ma et al. 2022; Z. Wang et al. 2023), conventional cross-correlation (CCF) ( N.A.Armand120
et al. 2003; M. Ma et al. 2022; Z. Wang et al. 2023), and wavelet coherence analysis (C. Torrence & G. P. Compo121
1998). Individual spikes provide a direct measure of the propagation delay of localized structures between spatially122
separated sampling points. The CCF obtain the function of time lag, combining information from different fluctuation123
scales, whereas wavelet-based correlation resolves the scale-dependent correlations in both time and scale. Coherent124
disturbances were identified using regions with high coherence (wavelet cross-correlation coefficient≥0.8). The125
projected propagation speed was calculated from the measured time delay and the projected baseline separation.126
127
2.3.MHD Simulation of wave reflection128
We interpret and reproduce the counter-propagating signatures in the observed density fluctuations within a wave-129
reflection scenario. Wave reflection can occur in the presence of gradients in the background density and magnetic130
field. We use a MHD simulation in a one-dimensional magnetic flux tube. Specifically, in the background flow, a131
sinusoidal velocity perturbation is imposed at the upstream boundary and propagate through a localized density132
enhancement representing an inhomogeneous coronal structure. The plasma parameters and magnetic-field strength133
are constrained by the MHD wave-speed inferred from the multi-station measurements. The governing equations and134
key model parameters are described in Appendix B, the simulation data can be found in L. Yuan (2026).135
136

the outer corona, we employ a multi-stage diagnostic pipeline combining time–frequency decomposition, multi-baseline139

3.2.Kinematic and numerical signatures of wave reflection184
Multi-baseline radio sounding of inward-propagating compressive disturbances reveals that near-Sun density fluctu-185
ations are not merely frozen-in structures convected by the solar wind, but also contain compressive waves propagating186
relative to the background medium (S. A. Jacques 1977; R. L. Dewar 1970). The long Yg–Hh baseline clearly re-187
solves two outward-propagating components, with no apparent contribution from the inward-propagating disturbance.188
We therefore identify the slow component,v bg = 143 km s −1, as the background flow and the fast component,189
vf = 370 km s−1, as a wave propagating outward through this flow. In the inertial frame, the latter can be expressed190
asv f =v bg +v w, wherev w is the wave propagation speed in the plasma rest frame, yieldingv w = 237 km s −1. If191
the inward-propagating component is the reflected counterpart of this outward-propagating wave, its propagation192
direction reverses in the plasma rest frame, such that +vw becomes−v w. Transforming back to the inertial frame then193
givesv in =v bg −v w = 143−237 =−94 km s −1.The negative sign indicates sunward propagation in the inertial frame.194
Remarkably, this predicted velocity agrees closely with the observed inward velocity of∼ −90 km s −1, providing195
quantitative support for the interpretation of the inward component as a reflected compressive wave.196
197
To further investigate the origin of the inward-propagating component, we perform one-dimensional MHD simula-198
tions of wave reflection by a localized density enhancement. Two virtual stations are placed along the radial direction199
with a separation of 4000 km, corresponding to the projected length of the Bd–Hh baseline. The observed wave speed200
(vw = 237 km s −1) and background-flow speed (v bg = 143 km s −1) are adopted in the simulation. The simulation201
reproduces three key features consistent with the observations.202
203
First, the simulated fast outward and inward components propagate at approximately 380 and−94 km s −1, respec-204
tively, consistent with the observed velocities and the kinematic relation expected for wave reflection (Fig. 5).205
206
Second, around the transition from outward to inward propagation (06:30 UTC), the simulated phase fluctuations207
generated from wave compressive and rarefaction exhibit features similar to those observed (shown by dashed box in208
Fig. 4c). The simulations further demonstrate that these phase signatures can arise from nonlinear wave steepening209
triggered by interface reflections. The resulting steepened velocity profiles subsequently generate corresponding density210
variations through the continuity equation. Fourier analysis of the observed phase fluctuations reveals higher-order211
harmonics, with three dominant frequencies at 0.44, 0.66, and 1.11 mHz (Fig. 4e). Their nearly integer frequency212
ratios are consistent with harmonic generation during the nonlinear evolution of propagating magnetosonic waves.213
The simulated phase fluctuations likewise exhibit harmonics up to the fifth order, with the fourth and fifth harmonics214
dominating at 0.60 and 0.75 mHz, respectively. Although the dominant harmonic orders differ, the presence of215
higher-order harmonics in both cases suggests that the wave interactions and nonlinear evolution contribute to the216
observed phase structures.217
218
Since the second-, third-, and fifth-order harmonic components dominate the phase-fluctuation spectrum, we approx-219
imate the observed phase fluctuation, ∆ρ(x, t), using a three-mode representation that retains these three dominant220
harmonics while neglecting the remaining components.221
∆ρ(x, t) =A 1 cos [2(kx−ωt)] +A 2 cos [3(kx+ωt) +ϕ 2] +A 3 cos [5(kx+ωt) +ϕ 3] +. . .(1)222
whereA n andϕ n denote the amplitude and phase shift of then-th harmonic, respectively. Note that a mode propa-223
gating in the positive x-direction has a minus sign in front ofωt, and vice versa. Depending on their relative phases,224
the higher-order harmonics interfere either constructively or destructively, resulting in localized density compression225
or expansion (Fig. 4c and Fig. 5). Whether the wave in the compression or rarefaction is determined by the inter-226
ference pattern, rather than by its direction of propagation. Although finite-amplitude outward-propagating waves227
can also undergo nonlinear steepening, the coexistence of counter-propagating signatures and wave steepening in both228
observation and simulation suggests that wave reflection likely contributes to the observed behavior.229

Third, the observed propagation transitions from predominantly outward to inward near the wave-compression re-231

3.3.Recurrence of reflection-like events249
Another similar event was observed on 18 October 2021 at a heliocentric distance of 12.33R ⊙, where the ray250
path crossed a streamer. Three independent baselines (Zc–Ks, Zc–Bd, and Ys–Zc) consistently detected coexisting251
outward- and inward-propagating disturbances (Fig. 6a–c). Wavelet cross-correlation reveals a coherent outward-252
propagating wave packet during 07:48–08:10 UT (250∼400 s), followed by an inward-propagating wave packet during253
08:12–08:36 UT. The coexistence of outward- and inward-propagating components is also evident in the conventional254
cross-correlation analysis. All three baselines show a primary peak around 7 s, corresponding to the fast outward-255
propagating component, while the Zc–Ks and Ys–Zc baselines additionally exhibit secondary peaks at 20∼26 s and256
-12∼-8 s, corresponding to the slow outward- and inward-propagating components, respectively (Fig. 6d).257
258
The cross-correlation strength depends strongly on the orientation of the projected baseline. The Zc–Ks baseline,259
with the best radial alignment, exhibits the strongest correlation, consistent with the expected predominantly radial260
propagation of the fluctuations. From the Zc–Ks baseline, the fast outward- and inward-propagating speeds are esti-261
mated to be 241 km s −1 and−150 km s −1, respectively, while the Ys–Zc baseline yields a slow outward-propagating262
speed of 64 km s −1.263
264
Interpreting the slow outward component as the background solar wind gives a wave speedv w of approximately265
177 km s −1 in the plasma rest frame. The corresponding inward-propagating speed,v in =v bg −v w, is−113 km s −1 in266
the inertial frame, differing from the observed value of−150 km s −1 by only∼40 km s −1. The larger velocity-closure267
uncertainty on Oct. 18 mainly arises from the coexistence of bidirectional propagation components. As cross-268
correlation represents a statistical average over the analysis interval, disturbances propagating in different directions269
introduce additional uncertainty into the derived velocities. Nevertheless, the observed velocities remain approximately270
consistent with the kinematic relation expected for wave reflection, supporting a reflection interpretation of the inward271
component.272
273
Extending the wavelet-coherence analysis to all 18 observing sessions reveals coexisting inward- and outward-274
propagating fluctuations in eight cases spanning different heliocentric distances (Appendix A, Table 1), indicating that275
inward propagation is not confined to an isolated event. The cases on Oct.4 and Oct.18 in 2021 exhibit sufficiently well-276
separated cross-correlation peaks to independently resolve the fast outward, slow outward, and inward-propagating277
components, allowing a quantitative velocity-closure test of the wave-reflection scenario.278

4.DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSION280
4.1.Applicability of the Taylor frozen-in hypothesis281
The propagation velocities derived in this study rely on the Taylor frozen-in hypothesis (G. I. Taylor 1938; G. He282
et al. 2017), which relates temporal lags between spatially separated measurements to propagation velocities through283
v= ∆r/τ. Multi-point analysis reveals multiscale, intermittent, and anisotropic compressive fluctuations with both284
outward- and inward-propagating components. Nevertheless, the preservation of nearly identical spike-like pertur-285
bations between separated stations and the high wavelet coherence (WCOH≥0.8) (Fig. 2b,c,f,g) indicate sufficient286
spatial coherence to support the Taylor frozen-in approximation at specific scales. Conventional cross-correlation287
provides a statistical average across scales within the analysis interval. The combined use of multiple correlation288
methods across multiple independent baselines further supports the robustness of the derived propagation velocities.289
290
4.2.Wave reflection291
The observations support localized wave reflection as a plausible origin of the inward-propagating component. The292
observed outward and inward velocities approximately satisfy the kinematic relation expected for an incident–reflected293
wave pair. One-dimensional MHD simulations further reproduce the observed propagation reversal and phase struc-294

reflection efficiency, providing new constraints on the role of wave reflection in solar wind turbulence.311

4.3.Conclusion313
The coordinated multi-station radio sounding observation reveals multi-scale, intermittent, and bidirectional com-314
pressive fluctuations in the solar wind acceleration region. The inward-propagating components, together with their315
kinematic and phase characteristics, provide the first remote-sensing evidence for compressive-wave reflection in this316
region. These results demonstrate the potential of multi-station radio sounding for resolving the spatial evolution and317
propagation of compressive fluctuations in the inner solar wind.318
