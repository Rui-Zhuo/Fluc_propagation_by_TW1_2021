ABSTRACT
Wave reflection is widely invoked to generate counter-propagating fluctuations required for solar-wind23
turbulence, yet direct observational evidence in the outer corona remains scarce. Using coordinated24
multi-station radio sounding observations of Tianwen-1, we identify localized inward-propagating25
compressive disturbances coexisting with outward propagating fluctuations at 5.7 and 12.3R ⊙.26
The velocity relationship among fast/slow outward components and inward components satisfies the27
kinematic expectation of an incident–reflected wave pair. Magnetohydrodynamic simulations show28
that localized density inhomogeneities can partially reflect compressive waves, reproduce coherent29
inward-propagating wave packets and phase modulation structures similar to those observed. Together30
with the velocity closure, the observed propagation reversal and phase signatures closely reproduced31
by the MHD simulation provide a self-consistent observational evidence chain supporting localized32
wave reflection as the origin of the inward-propagating compressive fluctuations.33
34
1.INTRODUCTION35
The solar wind acceleration region, extending from a few to several tens of solar radii, governs the transfer of mass,36
momentum, and energy from the solar corona into the heliosphere (?). Despite decades of theoretical and observational37
efforts, how the solar wind continues to gain energy and accelerate through the outer corona remains one of the central38
unresolved problems in heliophysics (??).39
40
Among the proposed mechanisms for coronal heating and solar-wind acceleration, wave–turbulence driven models41
have emerged as one of the leading theoretical frameworks (?Perez et al. 2021). In this scenario, convective motions42

at the photospheric footpoints continuously inject Alfv´ enic fluctuations into the coronal magnetic field. As these43
fluctuations propagate outward through corona, large-scale gradients in density and Alfv´ en speed generate partial44
wave reflection, producing both outward and inward propagating components (Krogulec et al. 1994; Kumar et al.45
2025; Meyrand et al. 2025). Their nonlinear interaction drives a turbulent cascade that transfers energy from large46
scales to smaller scales, where dissipation heats the plasma and contributes to solar-wind acceleration. In addition to47
wave reflection, Parametric decay instability (PDI) is another promising mechanism linking Alfv´ en-wave dynamics to48
the generation of compressive fluctuations in the near-sun solar wind. PDI typically arises via non linear wave-wave49
coupling where the pump wave transfers energy to daughter waves while satisfying frequency and wave vector matching50
[9, 10]. The instability manifests as energy exchange between Alfv´en waves and slow or fast-mode magnetosonic51
waves, thereby amplifying turbulence cascades and facilitating effective energy transport [9, 10].52
53
Observational evidence for Alfv´ enic turbulence is abundant in the solar wind, and numerical models have successfully54
reproduced many observed properties of the corona and heliosphere. However, direct observational constraints on55
wave reflection and turbulence development within the solar-wind acceleration region remain limited. Measurements56
of propagating density fluctuations and their directional properties in the near-Sun plasma therefore provide important57
diagnostics for testing wave–turbulence driven models and quantifying their role in coronal heating and solar-wind58
acceleration.59
A fundamental requirement for MHD turbulence is the coexistence of oppositely propagating fluctuations (Morton60
et al. 2015; Perez et al. 2021). Nonlinear interactions between counter-propagating wave packets drive the turbulent61
cascade and facilitate the transfer of energy toward smaller scales, ultimately contributing to plasma heating and62
solar-wind acceleration (Bowen et al. 2018, 2025; Gonz´ alez et al. 2023). Reflection of outward-propagating waves63
by large-scale gradients in density, magnetic field strength, or Alfv´ en speed has long been proposed as the primary64
mechanism for generating inward-propagating fluctuations in the solar wind (Krogulec et al. 1994). Consequently,65
reflection-driven turbulence has become a cornerstone of modern theories of coronal heating and solar-wind acceler-66
ation (Kumar et al. 2025). However, despite its importance, direct observational evidence for wave reflection in the67
outer corona remains extremely limited. Most reported reflection signatures have been confined to the low corona68
(Zhou et al. 2022), where reflected slow-mode and Alfv´ enic disturbances have been identified in coronal loops, coronal69
holes, and chromospheric structures (Liu et al. 2014).70
71
Recent observations from Parker Solar Probe have revealed the coexistence of outward- and inward-propagating72
MHD fluctuations in the near-Sun solar wind, including substantial compressive components (Zhu et al. 2020; Zank73
et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2023). These measurements suggest that counter-propagating fluctuations may be common74
in the young solar wind (Zank et al. 2024). Nevertheless, single-point in-situ observations are challenge to determine75
whether inward-propagating disturbances originate from wave reflection, local generation processes, or nonlinear76
turbulent interactions (Raouafi N.E. 2023; Bourouaine and Perez 2018). Establishing the spatial origin of inward-77
propagating fluctuations therefore remains a major observational challenge. This challenge is particularly severe78
for compressive disturbances, which are expected to be spatially localized and often exhibit relatively weak density79
contrasts (Krogulec et al. 1994).80
81
Multi-station radio sounding provides a unique means of addressing this problem. Radio signals transmitted by82
deep-space spacecraft experience phase and frequency fluctuations as they propagate through density irregularities in83
the solar wind (Woo and Armstrong 1981; Wexler et al. 2020). By measuring propagation delays between multiple84
projected baselines, the propagation direction, velocity, and spatial extent of compressive disturbances can be directly85
constrained (Maoli Ma 2022). In this work, we analyze coordinated multi-station radio-sounding observations of86
the Tianwen-1 spacecraft obtained during the 2021 solar conjunction at heliocentric distances of 2.8–28.6R ⊙ . We87
identify localized inward-propagating compressive fluctuations coexisting with outward-propagating disturbances at88
heliocentric distances of 5.7 and 12.3R ⊙. Section 2.1 introduces the observation, Section??introduces the method,89
Section 3 describes the results and Section 4 is the discussion.90

2.DATA AND METHODS92
2.1.Multi-station Observing Campaign Design and Execution for Solar Conjunction93
To target the 2021 September-October solar conjunction, we specifically designed, coordinated, and executed an94
intensive multi-station radio-sounding campaign, conducting 18 dedicated observations of the Tianwen-1 spacecraft.95
The campaign leveraged antennas from the European VLBI Network (Schl¨ uter and Behrend 2007), Russian VLBI96
Network (Shuygina et al. 2019), Chinese VLBI Network (Liu et al. 2022), University of Tasmania (Molera Calves et al.97
2021; Edwards et al. 2025), and Chinese Kashi and Jiamusi deep-space stations (Zhang et al. 2022)(Fig. 1a,b, Table 198
in Appendix A).99
100
These purpose-driven multi-station observations cover heliocentric distances from 2.8 to 28.6R ⊙ and heliographic101
latitudes from 0.1 ◦ to 67.1◦, with eight sessions sampling within 10R ⊙, where fine-scale compressible fluctuations are102
expected to be most dynamically active.103
For each observing station, the closest approach point of the spacecraft radio ray to the Sun was determined and104
projected into the Carrington coordinate system (The SunPy Community et al. 2020). The projected separation105
between two stations defines the projected baseline (Fig. 1c). The joint observations of multiple ground stations form106
time-varying projected baselines near the Sun (Fig. 1c), enabling multi-point turbulence measurements and directional107
discrimination of compressive fluctuation propagation with second-level temporal resolution.108
109
2.2.Propagation-Speed Measurements110
Residual frequency fluctuations were obtained after removing the Doppler trend from the spacecraft carrier signal.111
Propagation delays between stations were estimated using both cross-correlation and wavelet-coherence analyses112
(Torrence and Compo 1998; Maoli Ma 2022). Under the frozen-flow approximation (Taylor 1938), the projected113
propagation speed was calculated from the measured time delay and the projected baseline separation. To investigate114
scale-dependent propagation properties, wavelet coherence was applied to the frequency fluctuations (Torrence and115
Compo 1998). Coherent disturbances were identified using regions with high coherence (wavelet cross-correlation116
coefficient≥0.8).117
118
2.3.Parameter Constraints for the MHD Simulation119
Motivated by the unique multi-station observations, we further investigate the origin of the counter-propagating120
waves detected during the observing campaign using an MHD simulation in a one-dimensional magnetic flux tube.121
The plasma parameters and magnetic-field strength are constrained by the MHD wave-speed closure inferred from122
the multi-station measurements using the method described in Section 2.2. We interpret and reproduce the counter-123
propagating signatures in the observed density fluctuations within a wave-reflection scenario. Specifically, a sinusoidal124
velocity perturbation is imposed at the upstream boundary and allowed to propagate through a localized density125
enhancement representing an inhomogeneous coronal structure. The governing equations and key model parameters126
are described in Appendix B.127

The simulations are compared with the radio observations in three respects. First, the velocity relationship among158
the fast/slow outward component and the inward-propagating fluctuations is consistent with an incident-reflected wave159
pair rather than with unrelated transient structures. If the slower outward component identified on the Yg-Hh baseline160
is taken as the background flow velocity,v bg = 143 km s −1, the fast outward component satisfiesv out =v bg +v w,161
corresponding to an outward velocity of 380 km s −1 in the inertial frame (Fig. 4). The inward component correspond-162
ingly satisfiesv in =v bg −v w, yielding an inward velocity of 94 km s −1, in good agreement with the observed inward163
propagation speeds inferred from Fig. 2b and d, to within 15 km s −1. Second, the observed phase-modulation features164
within the superposition region is reproduced by the wave-reflection simulations, supporting an interference origin165
for the observed time-lag structures. The superposition of incident and reflected waves produces localized density166
compression and rarefaction, giving rise to phase patterns resembling the structures observed near 06:30 and 06:45167
UTC (Fig. 3c). Third, the local plasma-density ’bump’ near 06:30 UTC coincides with a transition from predominantly168
outward to inward propagation along the Bd–Hh baseline (Fig. 3b,d). This behavior is consistent with theoretical169
expectations that inward propagating perturbations are amplified near regions of enhanced wave compression, where170
reflected-wave energy reaches a maximum.171
172
Together, the consistency between the observations and simulations supports the hypothesis that magnetosonic173
wave reflections may serve as the most likely origin of the inward propagating compressive fluctuations.174

3.2.Bidirectional Propagation in Coronal Compressive Fluctuations176
Another similar case is identified on 18 October 2021 at a heliocentric distance of 12.33R ⊙, where the ray-path177
projection lies within a streamer structure. Multiple baselines (Zc-Ks, Zc-Bd, Ys-Zc, Ys-Ks, and Ys-Bd) consistently178
exhibit coexisting inward- and outward-propagating components. The velocities of the fast outward component and179
the inward-propagating component were derived from the most radial baseline (Zc–Ks), while the slow outward180
component was obtained from the neighboring Ys–Zc baseline (Fig. 5a,b). Interpreting the slow component as the181
background flow yields a wave speed of approximately 177 km s −1, while the inward component implies a comparable182
wave speed of approximately 214 km s −1. The two independent estimates differ by only a few tens km s −1, consistent183
with expectations from a reflection scenario. A weaker correlation enhancement is also visible at lags of approximately184
20–27 s on the Zc–Ks baseline (Fig. 5a), corresponding to a projected speed of 60–80 km s −1, broadly consistent with185
the slow outward component identified on the neighboring Ys–Zc baseline.186
187
Extending the wavelet-coherence analysis to all 18 observing sessions reveals that coexisting inward- and outward-188
propagating disturbances are present across nine sessions along the quasi-radial baselines (Appendix A), indicating189
that such behavior is not restricted to isolated events.190

4.DISCUSSION192
The observed coexistence of inward- and outward-propagating compressive disturbances is consistent with localized193
wave reflection in the solar-wind acceleration region. For the representative event on 2021 October 4, the velocity194
relationship among the fast/slow and inward components matches the kinematic expectation for an incident–reflected195
wave pair (Jacques 1977; Kumar et al. 2025) (Fig. 2 and 5). Beyond reproducing the inward-propagating wave196
signature, the simulation also generates localized compression and rarefaction patterns consistent with the observed197
phase structures, providing an independent consistency test of the reflection scenario (Fig. 3c,d and 4).198
199
Other mechanisms may also contribute to the generation of counter-propagating fluctuations. In particular, the200
parametric decay instability (PDI) of finite-amplitude outward-propagating Alfv´ en waves can generate backward-201
propagating Alfv´ enic daughter waves together with compressive density fluctuations (Bowen et al. 2018; Gonz´ alez202
